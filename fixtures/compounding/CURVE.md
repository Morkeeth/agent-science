# The compounding curve — the powered measurement

**56 claims across 4 independently written scripts on one subject, run in sequence into
one corpus.** Not an A→B pair; the shape a buyer actually experiences.

| leg | script | claims | from memory | searches | time | cost/claim |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | powered-A-law.txt | 15 | 0 (0%) | 11 | 273s | $0.00377 |
| 2 | documentary-orphan-works.txt | 10 | 2 (20%) | 6 | 153s | $0.00309 |
| 3 | powered-B-archive.txt | 18 | 7 (39%) | 14 | 254s | $0.00396 |
| 4 | documentary-orphan-works-B.txt | 13 | 6 (46%) | 9 | 198s | $0.00352 |

**Cumulative reuse: 15 of 56 = 27%.**

## What is true

**Reuse compounds, monotonically: 0% → 20% → 39% → 46%.** Four points, one direction, no
exceptions. This is the claim that was false at sentence-keying (9%) and unmeasurable at
n=7-11 (27–60%, three published corrections). At 56 claims it holds.

## What is NOT true, and it is the more interesting finding

**Cost per claim does not fall.** $0.00377 → $0.00352, flat, and
searches per claim is flat too (0.73 → 0.69).

**Reuse rises and cost does not, because the corpus removes the EASY claims first.** What
remains after reuse is a residue of hard ones — the claims that need escalation, a second
search aimed at a primary source. So each production checks a higher proportion of
difficult claims than the one before, and the per-claim spend holds while the hit rate
climbs.

That is a real property of the system, not noise, and it is invisible in a single A→B
ratio. **The saving a buyer gets is in claims they no longer have to chase by hand — not
in the API bill.**
