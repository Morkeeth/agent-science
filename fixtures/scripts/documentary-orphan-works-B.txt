NARRATOR (V.O.)

Every archivist has a shelf they walk past. Ours is on the second floor, and it has been
untouched since 2011.

Europe's answer arrived in 2012. Directive 2012/28/EU — the Orphan Works Directive —
told member states that a diligent search, properly recorded, was enough. They had until
29 October 2014 to write it into national law.

In Britain the Intellectual Property Office went first, opening an orphan works licensing
scheme in October 2014. It was the first scheme of its kind in Europe. In its opening
year it issued fewer than a hundred licences, which tells you either that the problem is
small or that the process is hard.

Washington took a different route, which is to say none at all. The United States
Copyright Office published Orphan Works and Mass Digitization in June 2015. It sits on a
shelf too.

The numbers are the part people do not believe. The British Library has estimated that
forty percent of its copyrighted collection is orphaned. The BFI National Archive holds
more than 275,000 titles. A study in April 2009 put the collections of UK public sector
organisations at around 25 million orphan works.

Canada, quietly, has done more than anyone. Under Section 77 of its Copyright Act the
Copyright Board can license an orphan work outright, and by June 2023 it had issued 321
such licences.

And in 2008, on the fourth of June, European representatives of museums, libraries,
archives and rightsholders signed a Memorandum of Understanding on orphan works. Then
the Google Books settlement, which would have built a registry for exactly this, was
rejected by a federal judge in 2011.
