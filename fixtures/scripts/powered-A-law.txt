NARRATOR (V.O.)

The law came late, and it came from Brussels.

In 2012 the European Union passed Directive 2012/28/EU, the Orphan Works Directive.
Member states were required to transpose it into national law by 29 October 2014. Its
core mechanism is the diligent search: if a rightsholder cannot be found after a
recorded, good-faith search, a cultural institution may digitise and publish the work.
The directive covers libraries, museums, archives, film heritage institutions and public
service broadcasters. Works cleared under it are entered in a single EU register
maintained by the European Union Intellectual Property Office in Alicante.

The United Kingdom went further and faster. The Intellectual Property Office opened an
orphan works licensing scheme in October 2014, the first national scheme of its kind in
Europe, and it issues licences for up to seven years, renewable. The UK scheme charges an
application fee and a licence fee, and the money is held in case a rightsholder appears.
In its first year the scheme granted fewer than one hundred licences.

The United States took the opposite path. The Copyright Office published Orphan Works and
Mass Digitization in June 2015, recommending a limitation on remedies rather than a
licensing body. Congress did not act on it. Earlier, the Shawn Bentley Orphan Works Act
passed the Senate in 2008 and died in the House. Section 108 of the US Copyright Act
still governs library and archive reproduction, and it was written in 1976.

Canada is the quiet exception. Under Section 77 of its Copyright Act the Copyright Board
of Canada can issue a licence for a published work whose owner cannot be located. By June
2023 the Board had issued 321 such licences and denied 36 applications.

The private sector tried first and failed. The Google Books settlement would have created
a Book Rights Registry covering orphan works; Judge Denny Chin rejected it in March 2011.
The Authors Guild litigation that followed ended in 2016 when the Supreme Court declined
to hear the case, leaving the Second Circuit's fair use ruling standing.

Before any of it, on 4 June 2008, European representatives of libraries, archives,
museums and rightsholders signed a Memorandum of Understanding on Diligent Search
Guidelines for Orphan Works. It was voluntary. It was also the moment the problem was
first written down by the people who had it.
