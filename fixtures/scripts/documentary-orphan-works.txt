NARRATOR (V.O.)

Somewhere in a temperature-controlled vault outside London sit reels nobody is allowed
to touch. Not because they are fragile. Because nobody knows who owns them.

The European Union tried to fix this. In 2012 it passed the Orphan Works Directive,
Directive 2012/28/EU, and member states were required to bring it into national law by
29 October 2014. The idea was simple: if you search diligently and cannot find the
rightsholder, a library or an archive may digitise the work anyway.

Britain went further. The Intellectual Property Office launched an orphan works
licensing scheme in October 2014, the first of its kind in Europe, and in its first year
it granted fewer than one hundred licences.

Across the Atlantic the answer was a shrug. The United States Copyright Office published
its report, Orphan Works and Mass Digitization, in June 2015, and Congress did nothing
with it.

The scale is not small. The British Library has estimated that forty percent of its
copyrighted collection is orphaned. The BFI National Archive holds more than 275,000
titles, and a significant fraction of the amateur and industrial film in it has no
traceable owner at all.

And the money already tried once. The Google Books settlement, which would have created
a registry for exactly these works, collapsed in 2011 when a federal judge rejected it.
